# How Marleigh's Math Mind Works

## A Garvey Genius Map reading

### What this report is

This is a learner-centered reading based on Marleigh's voluntary Math Genius Discovery conversation. It describes patterns she recognized in her own words. It is not an IQ score, a diagnosis, an academic placement decision, or a permanent label. A fuller Garvey profile should keep four sources separate and let them inform one another over time:

1. Marleigh's own reflection.
2. Parent and teacher observation.
3. Evidence from math work and explanations.
4. Evidence from practice activities over time.

## Her learner statement

> **Help me understand the idea deeply; then let me test it, compare it, vary it, and use it to build something real.**

That sentence is the most useful starting point for adults planning Marleigh's math experience.

## The seven Genius Map categories

The Garvey Genius Map does not claim a child is one fixed “type.” These categories describe strengths that can appear alone or combine in a situation.

### 1. Thinking & Problem Solving

**What this category means:** Seeing structure in a challenge: breaking it apart, noticing patterns, comparing options, and explaining why a method fits.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She begins with the part she understands; uses a solved problem as a reference; asks whether a new method works for every problem; and considers efficiency.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Decomposes a confusing task instead of treating it as one giant obstacle.
- Looks for transfer: “Will this work on another kind of problem?”
- Is interested in method choice, not only the final answer.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Build a deliberate **method-selection habit**. Before solving, she can name two possible approaches, choose one, and explain why it fits. Afterward, she can ask when the other approach would have been better.

**Best environment:**

- A clear, challenging problem with time to think.
- Two worked methods that can be compared, not merely copied.
- Visual models, tables, diagrams, or examples that make the structure visible.
- A prompt such as: “What is the same? What changed? Which method is more useful here, and why?”

**Signs this strength is active:** Breaking a problem into parts; noticing a pattern; checking whether a rule generalizes; comparing efficiency; explaining a choice of strategy.

### 2. Curiosity & Love of Learning

**What this category means:** Wanting to investigate, ask questions, and understand beyond the first answer.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She requests a deeper explanation when a new concept does not click. She wants to work related problems to understand the concept and see its different forms.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Asks for understanding, not answer rescue.
- Wants to know how and why a method works.
- Keeps learning after an initial success.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Turn questions into a short inquiry routine: **question → prediction → test with an example → explain what changed.** This will help her curiosity become evidence-based engineering thinking.

**Best environment:**

- Adults welcome “why?” and show the underlying reasoning.
- One new concept at a time, with enough worked steps to make it visible.
- A question bank or “wonder list” where she can save questions that deserve later research.
- Permission to test a rule on several examples instead of accepting it only because an adult said it.

**Signs this strength is active:** Asking for a deeper explanation; seeking another example; wondering whether a method always works; continuing to investigate after the answer is known.

### 3. Creativity & Idea Thinking

**What this category means:** Generating possibilities, making useful connections, designing new approaches, and improving an idea beyond its first version.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She wants visually designed learning through Canva, imagines multiple methods for different problems, and connects mathematical learning to an H.A. robot that could help seniors remain independent.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Sees mathematics as a tool for design and invention.
- Wants learning materials that are visual and personally meaningful.
- Understands that different problems may call for different methods.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Pair idea generation with a **test-and-revise cycle**. Each new method should answer: What problem is it for? What assumptions does it make? Where does it work? Where does it fail or need revision?

**Best environment:**

- Open-ended design problems with a real purpose.
- Time to sketch, storyboard, build slides, create a table, or make a model before formal calculation.
- Adults who ask for three possible approaches before selecting one.
- A visible revision cycle: idea → prototype → test → revise → explain.

**Signs this strength is active:** Creating a new representation; suggesting a different method; redesigning a lesson; connecting a school skill to an invention or real-world need.

### 4. Effort & Resilience

**What this category means:** Staying engaged through a productive challenge, recovering after an error, and returning to a goal with a useful next move.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She enjoys the moment a hard problem becomes understandable, laughs off a missed number, moves to harder questions once ready, and accepts prerequisite work as the path to a valued goal.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Does not treat one error as a verdict on her ability.
- Chooses increased challenge voluntarily.
- Can connect less-exciting prerequisite work to a future reward.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Learn to name the **specific obstacle** before pushing through it: missing concept, missed detail, unclear representation, or strategy mismatch. This keeps persistence from becoming repeated effort with the same unhelpful approach.

**Best environment:**

- Problems that are meaningfully challenging but have accessible entry points.
- Feedback that recognizes strategy, revision, and recovery—not only correct answers.
- A visible progress path from “what I know” to “what I am learning” to “what I can now do.”

**Signs this strength is active:** Retrying after an error; choosing a harder related problem; naming a future goal that makes the prerequisite worth doing; returning to a challenge with a changed strategy.

### 5. Learning From Feedback

**What this category means:** Using a hint, correction, example, or coaching note to make the next attempt more accurate or more thoughtful.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She traces an error, asks for deep step-by-step explanation, and keeps a solved problem as an example for the next related problem.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Looks for the cause of an error.
- Knows when she needs more than a quick hint.
- Carries an example forward into a new task.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Move from using an example to **explaining the principle without looking at it.** After one supported problem, she can cover the example and say: “The reason this step comes next is…”

**Best environment:**

- Specific feedback tied to one step or one decision.
- A worked example followed by a closely related independent problem.
- Time to explain the correction in her own words.
- Corrections framed as information for the next attempt, not as a judgment.

**Signs this strength is active:** Locating an error; asking a useful question; changing a next attempt because of a hint; using a worked example independently and then fading reliance on it.

### 6. Focus & Self-Control

**What this category means:** Directing attention, organizing the next step, and shaping a work routine that makes sustained learning possible.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** She varies related practice instead of repeating one type until bored. She raises difficulty when she has the hang of it and can tolerate prerequisite work when it leads to a meaningful goal.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Notices boredom before it becomes disengagement.
- Creates variation and progression for herself.
- Can organize present steps around a future goal.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Use a short **learning plan before practice**: today’s concept, number of examples, one variation, one stretch problem, and a final reflection. This makes her self-direction reproducible across subjects.

**Best environment:**

- A visible sequence and an understandable first step.
- Short work cycles with planned variation, not a long page of identical problems.
- Choice within structure: choose a model, a context, or the final stretch challenge while keeping the learning goal fixed.

**Signs this strength is active:** Choosing a start point; using a plan; changing practice format to sustain engagement; moving to a stretch problem at the right moment.

### 7. Interests & Passion Areas

**What this category means:** The activities, problems, and subjects a learner returns to and wants to develop through repeated engagement.

**How Marleigh fits it now:** Her H.A. robot idea combines robotics, engineering, helping seniors, design, and mathematics. She also wants visual authorship over her math learning materials.

**Strengths to build on:**

- Has a meaningful real-world reason to learn harder material.
- Connects mathematical ideas to technology that can help people.
- Sees long-term learning as preparation for invention.

**Growth edge — what to practice next:** Convert the H.A. robot vision into a sequence of small, documentable projects so interest becomes a portfolio of tested skills rather than a distant idea.

**Best environment:**

- Purpose-driven projects with an authentic user or problem.
- Math lessons connected to robotics: measurement, coordinate geometry, ratios, estimation, data, logic, coding, sensors, and physical design.
- A portfolio where she can keep sketches, calculations, tests, revisions, and explanations.

**Signs this strength is active:** Returning to robot ideas; connecting a concept to an invention; voluntarily researching prerequisites; choosing practice that advances a meaningful project.

## Learning plan: evidence-aligned and personalized

### Weekly Math-and-Invention Lab (three 35–45 minute sessions)

**Session 1 — Learn the idea deeply**

1. Introduce one concept with a visual model and one worked example.
2. Ask Marleigh to explain each step in her own words: “Why is this step here?”
3. Give one near-transfer problem using the same underlying structure.
4. End with: “What stays the same if the numbers or story change?”

**Session 2 — Vary, mix, and choose a method**

1. Mix several related and previously learned problem types.
2. Have Marleigh identify the kind of problem before calculating.
3. Offer two legitimate methods when possible.
4. Ask her to choose a method, explain the choice, and solve.
5. Add one stretch problem only after she identifies why she is ready for it.

**Session 3 — Build it into the H.A. robot**

1. Pick one narrow robot question: How far must the arm reach? How will it recognize a voice command? How can it avoid an obstacle?
2. Make a simple representation: sketch, measurement table, flowchart, coordinate grid, or slide.
3. Identify the math or science prerequisite.
4. Test one small idea and record what happened.
5. End with one question for the next session.

### Why these practices fit

- Marleigh already uses worked examples as references. Research on self-explanation while studying examples supports prompting learners to explain why steps work, so examples become transferable understanding rather than objects to copy. [Chi et al., 1989]
- She already seeks varied related questions. In an experiment with college mathematics learners, spaced and mixed practice produced stronger delayed performance than blocked same-type practice. This supports testing a structured mixed-practice approach with her; it does not guarantee the same effect for every learner or every topic. [Rohrer & Taylor, 2007]
- She values understanding and error tracing. Studies of mathematics feedback and worked examples have examined prompting learners to inspect, explain, and correct errors instead of receiving only an answer. [McLaren et al., 2021; Adams et al., 2014]
- She benefits from personal design and meaningful projects. Longitudinal research in school mathematics has found associations between teacher motivational style, interest, achievement, and mathematics self-concept. The Garvey use of choice is therefore **structured choice inside a clear learning goal**, not choice without teaching. [Valas & S\u00f8vik, 1993]

## What to avoid

- Do not give only a page of identical problems after she understands the first few.
- Do not mistake a request for deeper explanation as avoidance; it is a request for the missing structure she needs to proceed independently.
- Do not praise her only for being “smart.” Name the method, decision, revision, and persistence she used.
- Do not make the H.A. robot an abstract reward far in the future. Use small robotics-linked math challenges now.
- Do not turn this report into a ceiling. Her profile can change as she encounters new content, environments, relationships, and evidence.

## Practical language for adults

Instead of: “You are good at math.”

Say: “You noticed that this method may not fit every problem. Let’s test the conditions where it works.”

Instead of: “Just keep trying.”

Say: “What kind of obstacle is this: a missing step, a new rule, a detail you missed, or a strategy that does not fit?”

Instead of: “Here is the answer.”

Say: “Here is one example. Tell me what each step is doing, then try a new version without looking.”

Instead of: “Finish this worksheet.”

Say: “Choose three varied examples, one method comparison, and one stretch problem. Then tell me what changed.”
